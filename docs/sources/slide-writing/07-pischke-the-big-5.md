## Core Slide-Writing Principles

### Direct source claims

- The guide presents its advice as suggestions rather than rules, while arguing that the combined system is a reliable way to make a seminar clear. Source: [PDF, pp. 1-2](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=1).
- The central "Big 5" system says the introduction should immediately cover five items: the research question, why it matters, what is deficient or missing in prior work, what the presenter does better, and the main results. Source: [PDF, pp. 1-2](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=1) and [PDF, pp. 5-6](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=5).
- The first five minutes are treated as the most critical part of the seminar, because that is when the speaker sets the audience's expectations and should tell them where the paper is going. Source: [PDF, p. 5](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=5).
- The research question should be stated immediately, in plain English, and roughly within 25 words. The source warns against starting by slowly backing into the question through a chart, statistics, or vague motivation. Source: [PDF, pp. 6-7](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=6).
- The motivation should answer why anyone should care, including why the issue matters for economic understanding or for the economy and policy. Source: [PDF, pp. 7-8](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=7).
- The literature discussion should focus on gaps, especially the gap the paper fills, rather than reviewing the whole literature. Source: [PDF, pp. 8-9](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=8).
- The value-added item should state what is new, different, and better. For empirical work, the source gives examples such as a new data set, new technique, or old technique applied to a new setting. Source: [PDF, pp. 9-10](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=9).
- The main result should be summarized up front. The source explicitly rejects suspense-driven seminar structure and says audiences should get the central takeaway near the beginning. Source: [PDF, pp. 10-11](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=10).
- Early-stage projects still need a takeaway. If definitive results are not available, the presenter can show a template result table, identify the crucial estimates, explain possible signs, and say how different empirical outcomes would be interpreted. Source: [PDF, p. 11](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=11).
- The Big 5 should ideally take about five minutes, with minimal jargon, concrete examples, few extraneous details, no digressions, and repetition where needed to ensure understanding. For data-oriented talks, early numbers should be reduced to directional or magnitude categories such as big, small, positive, or negative. Source: [PDF, pp. 12-13](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=12).
- After the Big 5, the presenter should solicit clarification questions, but defer tangents until later so the talk does not lose its path before the core idea is established. Source: [PDF, p. 13](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=13).
- The guide warns against moving from a clear introduction directly into dense equations, full tables, or complex charts. The problem is not rigor itself, but "too much, too soon." Source: [PDF, pp. 13-17](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=13).
- After the Big 5, the audience needs an "intuitive appetizer": a simple intuition or story that prepares them for the technical material. The source argues that this moment may work best without slides, or with only the simplest sketch, because everyone should be focused on the presenter and the idea. Source: [PDF, pp. 17-22](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=17).
- Once the Big 5 and intuitive appetizer are in place, the presenter can proceed to technical details because the audience now knows what the equations, data, or model are for. Source: [PDF, pp. 22-23](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=22).
- The source recommends flagging important limitations early, either in the takeaway or near the end of the intuitive appetizer, so the audience understands what is unfinished and can help improve the work. Source: [PDF, pp. 23-24](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=23).
- Slides are described as valuable and often expected, especially for charts, graphs, and empirical visual displays; the source says a visual distribution plot can be more useful than many regression coefficients. Source: [PDF, p. 24](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=24).
- The source's slide pitfalls include tiny fonts, too many digits, Greek-letter overload, dense text read aloud word for word, and slow covered reveals. For reveals, it suggests separate slides or speaking directly instead. Source: [PDF, pp. 24-25](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/spischke/phds/The%20Big%205.pdf#page=24).

### Interpretation for economic research talks

- The source is mainly about seminar architecture, not visual style. Its practical message is that an economics audience should receive the question, contribution, answer, and intuition before being asked to process the paper's technical apparatus.
- The "Big 5" is a job-market and seminar opening template. It makes the first minutes perform the work that weak talks often postpone: defining the estimand or question, making the stakes concrete, locating the literature gap, stating the contribution, and revealing the main result.
- The "intuitive appetizer" is a bridge between a persuasive opening and the technical core. In empirical economics, this bridge can be a simple example of the mechanism, a one-cell version of the identification idea, a verbal DAG-style story, or a minimal institutional example before the full model or table appears.
- The source is strongly anti-mystery: it treats suspense as bad for seminars because audience members need the destination in order to interpret details.
- The source is not anti-technical. It argues for staging technical material so the audience can appreciate it, rather than seeing it before they know why it matters.
- The literature-gap language should be translated charitably in current decks. "Deficient prior work" can become "what remains unresolved," "what existing work cannot identify," or "the gap this design addresses."

## Beamer-Specific Implications

- Build the first substantive Beamer sequence around the Big 5:

```tex
% Opening sequence pattern
% 1. Question: plain-English research question
% 2. Stakes: why this matters for economics, policy, or measurement
% 3. Gap: what existing work leaves unresolved
% 4. Contribution: what this paper does differently
% 5. Takeaway: main result, plus one major caveat if needed
```

- Do not open an economics talk with an unexplained graph, summary-statistics table, or equation. If a figure appears in the opening, the frame title and spoken setup should already make clear what question the figure answers.
- Use substantive frame titles for the Big 5, for example `Question: do transfers persist across generations?`, `Gap: two-generation data cannot identify traditions`, or `Takeaway: effects persist after conditioning on wealth`.
- Keep the Big 5 concise. In Beamer, this often means one tight frame per item for a full seminar, or a compressed two-frame version for a short conference talk.
- Add an intuition bridge immediately after the Big 5 and before the first dense method or result frame. This can be a blank/no-slide moment, a single-example frame, a simple schematic, or a minimally annotated equation with most notation deferred.
- When showing a model equation, display the economic object first and the notation second. A useful Beamer pattern is to show the intuition in words, then the stripped-down equation, then only later the full model or first-order conditions.
- When showing results early, reduce numbers to their role in the argument: sign, rough magnitude, or direction. Exact coefficients, many controls, and multiple columns belong after the intuition or in backup.
- For preliminary work, include a table shell or hypothesis grid that shows where the decisive coefficient will appear and how different signs would be interpreted. This makes the "takeaway" possible even before final estimates exist.
- Use backup slides for full regression tables, derivations, robustness checks, and tangent-prone details. The main deck should protect the Big 5 and intuition before exposing the audience to technical density.
- Avoid Beamer mechanics that reproduce the source's criticized slide habits: tiny fonts, tables with unnecessary digits, slides packed with Greek letters, text slides that invite reading aloud, and slow hidden-content reveals. Prefer separate build-up frames, highlighted rows/cells, or direct explanation.
- Include an early limitations cue for seminar-length talks. A concise caveat in the takeaway or intuition bridge can preempt unproductive gotcha questions and invite more useful feedback.

## What This Would Change In Our Current Framework

- Add a named "Big 5 opening" checklist to the slide-writing rules: question, stakes, gap, contribution, and takeaway should appear before dense technical detail in a full research seminar.
- Add an "intuitive appetizer" convention: after the opening, include a simple intuition bridge before equations, full result tables, or complex charts. The bridge may be a spoken/no-slide moment.
- Add an early-stage-work pattern: if results are not final, use a table shell, sign predictions, or interpretation grid so the audience still gets a clear takeaway.
- Add a rule against opening by sneaking up on the question through statistics, charts, or institutional background. The research question should be explicit first.
- Add a slide audit item for the source's five common failures: tiny font, excessive digits, unexplained Greek notation, dense text read aloud, and slow covered reveals.
- Add a caveat-placement guideline for seminar talks: one major limitation can be disclosed early, near the takeaway or intuition bridge, when it helps calibrate the audience and elicit useful feedback.

## Tensions Or Caveats

- The PDF is from 2000 and predates contemporary Beamer norms. Its "no slides" advice for the intuition bridge should not be read literally for every talk; a modern version can be a blank frame, a simple title-only frame, or one clean schematic.
- The guide is written primarily for PhD workshops, job-market preparation, and seminar culture. Short conference talks may need a compressed version of the Big 5 rather than five separate opening frames.
- The source itself says these are suggestions, not rules. The framework should adopt the architecture where useful without treating it as a universal script.
- The "deficiencies in previous work" wording can sound unnecessarily combative. Current economics decks should usually phrase this as an unresolved gap, limitation, or identification problem.
- Some parts of the PDF discuss attendance, seminar culture, listening to questions, and participant norms. Those are useful professional-development points, but they are outside this report's slide-writing scope.
- The assigned source label names Steve Pischke, while the document visibly lists Donald Cox. The safest attribution is "Donald Cox, hosted by Steve Pischke/LSE."
